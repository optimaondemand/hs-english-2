When an Argument Breaks
Lesson · Week 4 · Argument & Reasoning
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Some arguments lose because the evidence is thin. Others lose because the reasoning has a hole in it, and a hole can hide under a great deal of confidence.
In Lesson 4.03 you learned to state the other side’s strongest case before answering it. That skill assumes the other side has a real case. Sometimes it does not. Sometimes an argument only sounds strong, and an advocate who cannot say exactly where it fails has to either accept it or object without being able to explain why. Today you learn to name the failure precisely. You will find every example in this lesson inside the play you are already reading, because the characters in Oedipus the King argue badly under pressure in ways that are very recognizable.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell the difference between an argument that is weak and one whose reasoning is broken.
2
I will be able to name six common ways reasoning breaks, and point to each one in the play.
3
I will be able to explain why naming the break is only half of an objection, and supply the other half.
📚 PART 1 · WEAK IS NOT THE SAME AS BROKEN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time you knew someone was wrong but could not explain why in the moment, and only worked it out later. What was missing while you were standing there? (No wrong answer.)

Every argument you have built this module has three parts: a claim, evidence, and reasoning that connects the two. There are two very different ways that structure can fail, and an advocate has to answer them differently.

An argument is weak when the evidence is real and simply does not carry the claim as far as the claim goes. One matching detail in the herdsman’s account is real evidence. It is not yet enough to convict. You answer a weak argument by showing how much more it would need to prove.

An argument is broken when the reasoning itself does not connect, so more evidence of the same kind would not fix it. If someone argues that Tiresias must be lying because he is bitter, then no amount of additional proof of Tiresias’s bitterness will establish that his accusation is false. Bitter people can be right. The link between the two claims was never there. You answer a broken argument by showing that the connection is missing.

The diagnostic question: if this person had twice as much evidence of exactly the same kind, would the argument work? If yes, it is weak. If no, it is broken, and you should be able to say where.
📚 PART 2 · SIX BREAKS, AND WHERE THEY HAPPEN IN THE PLAY

These six account for most broken reasoning you will meet, in a courtroom or anywhere else. Each has a name, and the name is useful for the same reason “epic simile” is useful: once you can say it, you can see it. Read the middle line of each card first, since that is the actual move. The example underneath comes from a scene you have already read.

📝 SIX WAYS REASONING BREAKS · WORKED FROM THE PLAY
GOING AFTER THE PERSON
The claim is never answered. The person who made it is discredited instead, as though that settled the claim.
Tiresias names Oedipus “the accursed polluter of this land.” Oedipus answers by calling him a fraud, a beggarly trickster, blind in his craft. Notice what is missing: any account of where Oedipus was the day Laius died.
TWO DOORS WHEN THERE ARE MORE
A situation with several possibilities is presented as a choice between exactly two, usually one absurd and one the speaker wants.
Oedipus concludes that either Tiresias is telling the truth or Tiresias and Creon are conspiring to take the throne. A third possibility never gets named: that Tiresias sincerely believes something mistaken, or that he is right and no plot exists at all.
TOO MUCH FROM TOO LITTLE
A conclusion far larger than the evidence gets drawn from one case, one detail, or one witness.
The crossroads location matches. That single agreement between two accounts is treated, in the moment, as though it settled everything about who killed Laius, before the number of attackers has been resolved at all.
AFTER IT, SO BECAUSE OF IT
Two things happen in sequence, and the earlier one is assumed to have caused the later one, with nothing but the order offered as proof.
Oedipus arrived, the Sphinx was defeated, Thebes prospered, so Oedipus must be favored by the gods. The sequence is accurate. The causal claim built on it is an assumption, and the play spends the rest of its length dismantling it.
THE CIRCLE
The thing being proved is quietly used as one of the reasons for proving it, so the argument never actually goes anywhere.
“Oedipus must be the polluter, because the plague proves the land is polluted, and the land is polluted because Oedipus is here.” Each half leans on the other. Nothing outside the circle is ever offered.
BECAUSE OF WHO SAID IT
The speaker’s standing is treated as proof rather than as a reason to take the claim seriously. This is the subtle one, because standing genuinely is evidence.
“Tiresias is a prophet, so the accusation is true.” His record is a real reason to weigh his words heavily. It is not the same as the account of the killing itself, which is what would actually establish the charge.

Notice how many of these Oedipus commits himself, and how sympathetic he is while committing them. He is not stupid and he is not lying. He is frightened, and frightened people reason badly in exactly these directions. That is worth remembering when you audit your own brief later in this lesson.

📚 PART 3 · NAMING IT IS ONLY HALF AN OBJECTION

Here is the trap that comes with learning these names, and it is worth more of your attention than the list itself. Once you can name a break, it is tempting to treat the name as the whole objection: to say “that is going after the person” and stop, as though a label were an argument. It is not. A label tells a reader what category the problem is in. It does not tell them what the problem is.

A complete objection has two parts. Name the break, then state what the argument would need in order to work. The second part is the part that actually does damage, and it is the part that shows you understood the other side rather than just catalogued it.

📝 HALF AN OBJECTION, THEN A WHOLE ONE
HALF · THE LABEL ALONE
My opponent argues that Oedipus is guilty because Tiresias says so. This is an appeal to authority.
WHOLE · THE LABEL PLUS WHAT IS MISSING
My opponent rests the charge on Tiresias’s standing as a prophet rather than on any account of the killing. Tiresias’s record is a strong reason to take the accusation seriously, and I do. But an accusation is not testimony about the event. To establish that Oedipus killed Laius, this argument would need the herdsman’s account of how many attackers there were, which is the one piece of evidence it never addresses.
Why this version does the damage: it grants what is genuinely strong, identifies the exact gap, and then names the specific evidence that would have to fill it. A Council member can act on this. They cannot act on a label.
⚡ TRY IT · WHERE DOES THE REASONING ACTUALLY BREAK?
An opposing advocate argues: “Oedipus cannot be trusted about the crossroads. He is a man who threatened an old blind prophet, insulted his own brother-in-law, and lost his temper twice in a single afternoon. A man like that will say anything to protect himself.” Four responses are offered below. Which one is a complete objection?
Which response is a complete objection?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
The first names the break correctly and then asks the Council to do the rest of the work. It never says what is missing or what would fill the gap, and “should be disregarded” is an instruction, not a reason. This is the label-as-objection trap in its purest form.
The third is tempting because it sounds more substantive, and it goes wrong in a different way: it claims character evidence has no place at all, which is too strong. How a man behaves under pressure can be genuinely relevant. The problem was never that character was mentioned. It was that character was offered as a substitute for an account of the killing.
The fourth answers a claim the opponent did not make. It argues about whether Oedipus is truly a violent man, which accepts the opponent’s framing and fights on their ground. Once you are debating his temper, you have conceded that his temper is what the case turns on.
📚 PART 4 · AUDIT YOUR OWN BRIEF

These six breaks are much easier to spot in someone else’s writing than in your own, for the same reason Oedipus cannot see his: you already believe your conclusion, so the steps toward it feel obvious. Run the four checks below against your Week 4 steelman and, later, against your draft brief.

CHECK ONE · DID I ANSWER THE ARGUMENT OR THE ARGUER?
Find every sentence in your brief about what kind of person Oedipus is. For each one, ask whether it does work your case actually needs, or whether it is standing in for evidence about the killing.
CHECK TWO · HAVE I LEFT OUT A THIRD OPTION?
Your brief argues guilty or not guilty by a standard you chose. Write down one possibility your framing excludes, then decide honestly whether you excluded it for a reason or because it was inconvenient. If it was inconvenient, address it.
CHECK THREE · DOES MY EVIDENCE REACH AS FAR AS MY CLAIM?
Take your strongest sentence and underline the claim, then underline the evidence. Say out loud what would have to be true for the second to establish the first. If you cannot say it, the connection is doing work you have not shown.
CHECK FOUR · AM I ASSUMING WHAT I AM PROVING?
Look at the standard of judgment you chose in Week 6’s planning. Does your argument establish that standard, or does it start from it? Starting from it is allowed, as long as you say so plainly. Presenting it as a conclusion when it was an assumption is the circle.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND BROKEN REASONING
Ask an AI tool to find the fallacies in a passage and it will return a confident list of names. Two known limits: it labels valid arguments as fallacies fairly often, because the surface pattern of a sound argument and a broken one can look identical, and it does not know which evidence your case actually needs, so it cannot supply the second half of an objection. If you use a tool here, treat every label it returns as a claim you have to verify against the passage yourself.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Weak arguments need more evidence. Broken arguments need a connection that was never there, and more evidence will not supply it. The diagnostic question sorts them: would twice as much of the same evidence fix this?

And when you find a break, do not stop at the name. Grant what is genuinely strong, say exactly where the reasoning fails, then name the evidence that would have to fill the gap. That is an objection. The name by itself is only a category.
