0. How to use this manual

You are facilitating an asynchronous, on-demand course for a large cohort (Optima designs to an approximately 500:1 student-teacher ratio). Every lesson, VR activity, and assignment stands alone for a solo student with no teacher online. Your job is not to deliver content live; it is to support and move each student forward around content that already teaches itself. As the course’s own build rule puts it: live teachers adapt and support around the same content, they do not rewrite it.

Read Sections 1–3 once to understand what students experience. Keep Sections 5–7 (gradebook, rubrics, answer guides) open while you grade. Use Sections 9–10 to plan your week and your heavy weeks. Sections 11–13 (standards, classical rationale, sensitive content) support reporting and family communication.

Conventions in this manual
  • [VERIFY] marks anything that could not be confirmed from the built Semester-1 course and should be checked against Optima’s live Canvas instance or the build team before you rely on it.
  • Tier pills refer to the optima-canvas-assignments four-tier system (Tier 1 practice/completion → Tier 4 wrappers). The manual explains the tiers; it does not redefine them.
  • Standards codes (Florida B.E.S.T. ELA.10.x) appear only in this teacher copy. They never appear on student pages.

2. Ungraded interactives and the intro/outro wrappers

Two features of this course are the ones facilitators most often misread. Both are working as designed.

2.1 Ungraded interactive activities cost you nothing

Every content lesson carries one or two embedded widgets: an early reflective check-in (ow-reflect, no wrong answer) and, after the teaching, an evaluative self-check (ow-select, with teaching feedback on each option). They run on the GitHub-hosted lesson page and give the student immediate feedback in the page.

  • They produce no submission and never hit the gradebook. There is nothing to grade and nothing to chase.
  • They are formation by practice, not busywork. A student who skips them does not show up as a missing grade; the gap surfaces indirectly, in weaker Quick Writes and project work.
  • Only ow-reflect and ow-select are implemented in this course. If you ever revise a lesson, do not add other widget types; there is no runtime for them.

Do not treat these as accountability points. Treat their downstream evidence (a Quick Write that misuses a craft move the self-check drilled) as your signal to reach out.

2.2 The intro/outro wrappers are Canvas Quizzes in survey mode

Each module opens and closes with a wrapper built as a Canvas Quiz in survey mode (not a wiki page). This is deliberate: survey responses become sortable cohort data across ~500 students. That analytics view is the entire point. Set each module to require its wrappers so you get one complete/incomplete signal per student.

WrapperWhat it captures
Module introQ1 essential-question first answer (essay); Q2 objectives acknowledgment (checkboxes); Q3 a “predict” multiple-choice (M1: which trait matters most; M2: does what he did or what he intended matter more).
Module outroQ1 the same essential question revisited (essay, “how has your answer changed”); Q2 a 1–5 confidence rating on the module’s core skill; Q3 “what’s still unclear” (short answer); Q4 a reflection on the module project.
The highest-leverage move in the whole course

Spend 15–20 minutes per module triaging the outro responses. It is the cheapest early-warning system you have.

What to look for: clusters of low confidence (1–2 on the Likert item); the same concept named repeatedly in “what’s still unclear” (e.g., many students unsure how to write a real counterclaim in M2); students whose revisited essential-question answer is blank or shows no movement; and simply who has not completed the wrapper at all.

What to do with it: post one targeted announcement to the whole section addressing the top recurring confusion; send a warm individual message to low-confidence or non-completing students; and, if a concept recurs, plan a short reteach note before the next module. Review the intro Q1 and outro Q1 side by side to see how thinking moved — do not read the outro alone.

The wrappers are byte-identical across Standard and Honors and contain no group-work opt-in (every activity in this course is solo).

3. AI and VR in this course (student-facing)

This section is the student-facing AI and VR picture, drawn from the built lessons. Your own AI tooling is separate and comes next, in Section 4.

3.1 AI: awareness, not required tool use

  • Posture. The course teaches AI awareness, not required use of any tool. Callouts are factual about what AI can and cannot do for a given task, and they stop there (no persuasion, no grand closers).
  • Where it appears. Gold/red “Worth knowing” callouts inside content lessons (e.g., Lesson 1.01: naming a convention is recall, deciding what it means is the reading only you can do) and on the assignment pages (the Bard’s Hall and Trial project pages each carry an AI note). A first-week “choosing an AI tool” note introduces the idea.
  • No tool is required. Students have M365, so Microsoft Copilot is named as the natural in-environment option, with other common, credible, free assistants named generically. The standing stance is verify and critique, never substitute. [VERIFY] Confirm the school-sanctioned AI tool list before pointing students to specifics; the build left this to be confirmed.
  • Do not add new AI requirements to the course. Speak to what is already there.

3.2 VR: solo, platform-agnostic, feeds the project

  • What students do. Two short solo VR activities per week, in a student-chosen space, each feeding the current module project. Examples: M1 — sketch/stage a “bard’s hall” telling-space, rehearse a scene aloud, premiere the telling; M2 — stage the plague-stricken altar, the crossroads, the tribunal, and rehearse the opening/closing advocacy.
  • Capture pattern. A short (about 60–90 second) device-agnostic capture: a screen/video recording with narration, audio plus photos/sketches, or a written description with images if recording is not possible. Saved to OneDrive and submitted through Canvas. Completion-graded, no rubric score.
  • How you review / assemble. These are quick completion glances. A natural teacher move is a periodic gallery walk: pull a handful of captures and share a few (with permission) in an announcement to build community around the work.
  • Platform-agnostic. Optima is moving to a new VR platform, so the pages name no platform and describe VR only by what the student does. Each VR page carries a short facilitator note (how to support, common sticking points) and defaults to student choice of space.
  • [VERIFY] The built VR pages carry a “note for build planning” banner: device/platform-specific step-by-step directions were to be added later. Confirm those steps are in place before the term begins.

4. Using Ignite AI to support students (teacher-facing)

[VERIFY] Confirm before relying on any feature below. Ignite AI’s exact feature names and availability vary by Canvas release and by what Optima has enabled on its instance. The capabilities below are described functionally; verify which are turned on for your account and do not promise students a button that is not enabled.

Ignite AI is the teacher’s Canvas AI assistant. It is deliberately kept separate from the student-facing AI posture in Section 3: you may lean on it freely as a support tool. Its purpose here is simple: make a 500:1 cohort feel personal without adding hours. Two rules govern everything below.

AI drafts; the teacher decides. You read, edit, and own anything sent to a student or family. AI never sends on its own.

Coach, not catch. AI is used to find students who need help and to speed warm, formative feedback. It is never used to surveil, rank, or auto-penalize.

Use Ignite AI to:

  • Draft individualized feedback you then edit and own. Paste a student’s Quick Write or project against the rubric in Section 6 and have Ignite AI draft next-step feedback (lead with what works, name one concrete move). Edit for accuracy and voice, then send. This is where it most speeds Tier 3 turnaround without changing your judgment.
  • Summarize cohort data fast. Have it summarize the outro-wrapper “what’s still unclear” free-text and the confidence distribution to surface the section’s top 2–3 sticking points before you write the weekly announcement.
  • Surface students who need a nudge. Ask it to flag low outro-confidence, missed Quick Writes, or a declining pattern across the weekly feeders, then draft a warm check-in message for each. You decide who to contact and what to say.
  • Generate practice variants. For a student who needs another rep on a Tier 1 skill (an in-medias-res opening, integrating a quotation, writing a fair counterclaim), have it produce a fresh practice prompt in the same shape as the week’s Quick Write.
  • Communicate with families. Draft clear, kind summaries of a student’s progress, and translate a message when that helps a family. You review and send.

8. Coach, not catch

Your job is to move each student forward, not to detect and punish. In practice:

  • Feedback names the next step, not just the error. Lead with what is working, then give one concrete move. “Your opening drops us right into the escape — strong. Next, cut the sentence of backstory in line 3 so nothing slows the entry.”
  • Allow re-attempts on Tier 1 practice. Quick Writes and self-checks are learning tools. Let a student revise and resubmit a feeder; the point is the skill, which then shows up in the project.
  • Use analytics and Ignite AI to find students who need help early, then reach out warmly — not to build a case against them. The outro-wrapper triage (Section 2) is your main early-warning tool.
  • On suspected AI misuse or academic dishonesty, open a conversation. Ask the student to talk through their thinking or their process. Treat it as formation, not a verdict. Avoid accusation-first workflows; the course’s AI stance is awareness and verify-and-critique, and the project pages already invite students to keep a short AI-use log.
  • Model good thinking in public, never single out weak work. If you share examples (a strong QW opening, a fair counterclaim), share them to teach the move, with permission.

12. What makes this course classical, and cultivating culture

12.1 What makes it classical

  • A primary-source encounter with great books. Students read whole works in real translations — the Odyssey (their own Fagles copy) and Sophocles’ Oedipus the King (public-domain Storr) — not excerpts or summaries. Reading is assigned by book and by scene landmark, keeping students inside the actual text.
  • The Great Conversation, held across works. One question runs through the year and visibly threads the two built modules: what does a community owe its king, and what does a king owe its community? The intro/outro wrappers make each student answer it, then answer it again, so they can see their own thinking move.
  • Notice, then name, then apply. Content lessons lead with a concrete moment (the suitors eating the king’s cattle; the invocation) before naming the convention, and the weekly Quick Write asks students to use the move in their own writing. The course teaches the skill, never the pedagogical label.
  • Imitation and disputation as the two project modes. M1’s Bard’s Hall is imitation (write and perform an Odyssey-style tale); M2’s Trial is disputation (argue a contested verdict, steelman the other side). Both are classical modes of learning by making and by arguing.
  • Wonder before mechanism. Lessons open with the strangeness of the thing (a poem sung from memory; a king who vows to hunt the cause of a plague not knowing it is himself) before the analytic tools arrive.

12.2 Cultivating culture in an asynchronous course

  • Make public what is usually private. With permission, share a strong Quick Write opening or a fair counterclaim in an announcement, framed to teach the move — this builds a sense of a class doing real work together.
  • Name students’ good thinking in feedback and announcements. In a 500-student async course, being seen is rare and powerful; specific praise (“your simile earned its length”) does more than a grade.
  • Model curiosity weekly. Let the announcement pose the week’s real question, not just its due dates.
  • Keep the virtue thread visible. The essential question is a question about justice, loyalty, and what we owe one another; return to it, especially at the outro.
  • Treat feedback as relationship. The coach-not-catch stance (Section 8) is the main way culture is built here: every message moves a student forward and assumes the best.
