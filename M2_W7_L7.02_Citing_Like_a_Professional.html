Citing Like a Professional
Lesson · Week 7 · Research & Citation
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Your brief cites two very different kinds of source: a 2,400-year-old play in translation, and a modern scholarly article. Each has its own correct MLA form.
You already know how to integrate a quotation into your own sentence. Today you learn how to credit it correctly: the in-text citation that tells a reader exactly where a quote came from, and the Works Cited entry that gives full publication information. You will need both forms for your brief, one for the play, one for your Week 5 JStor source, and one honesty rule governs both.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to cite a translated play in MLA in-text form and build its Works Cited entry.
2
I will be able to cite a scholarly journal article from JStor in MLA in-text form and build its Works Cited entry.
3
I will be able to explain why you only ever cite a source you have actually read yourself.
4
I will be able to recognize the three ways a writer takes someone else’s work without meaning to, and correct each one.
5
I will be able to build a paraphrase, a summary, and a citation from a source I gathered myself, and keep all three straight in my brief.
📚 PART 1 · CITE WHAT YOU ACTUALLY READ
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever repeated a fact you heard secondhand, only to realize later you were not sure where it originally came from? What would have made you more confident sharing it? (No wrong answer.)

A citation is a promise to your reader: I read this myself, and here is exactly where to find it. That promise only holds if it is true. Never cite a source you have not personally opened and read, even if an AI tool, a summary, or a classmate describes it accurately. If you cannot point to the actual page, article, or translation in your own hands, it does not belong in your Works Cited. This rule also settles a question students often ask about a translated play: you cite the translation you actually read, this Storr translation, not some other edition you have never seen.

📚 PART 2 · CITING A VERSE PLAY IN PROSE

This edition of the play has no page numbers or line numbers marked for you (that is why you have referred to scenes by shorthand all module: “the curse,” “the Tiresias confrontation”). MLA’s rule for a source like this is simple: when a source has no stable page or line numbers, do not invent one. Cite the author’s name alone.

IN-TEXT CITATION
Oedipus extends his curse to include himself “if with my privity he gain admittance to my hearth” (Sophocles).
Cite the playwright, Sophocles, not the translator. The translator is credited fully in the Works Cited entry below, not repeated in every in-text citation.
WORKS CITED ENTRY
Sophocles. “Oedipus the King.” The Oedipus Trilogy, translated by F. Storr, Project Gutenberg, 2000, www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31.
Notice the pieces: the playwright, the specific play in quotation marks (since it is one work inside the larger collected ebook), the collection title italicized, the translator named, the publisher, the year, and the URL.
📚 PART 3 · CITING YOUR JSTOR SOURCE

A scholarly journal article works differently: it does have stable page numbers, so your in-text citation names both the author and the exact page a quote or idea came from. The example below is a model, built to show the correct form; it is not a real article to look up. Use it to check the shape of your own citation, built from the actual source you logged in Quick Write 13.

IN-TEXT CITATION (MODEL)
One scholar argues that ancient audiences judged guilt by the act itself, since “pollution asks what was done, not what was known” (Bennett 220).
Author’s last name, then the page number, no comma between them, no word “page” or abbreviation.
WORKS CITED ENTRY (MODEL)
Bennett, Laura. “Guilt Without Knowledge: Pollution and Intent in Sophoclean Tragedy.” Classical Quarterly Review, vol. 42, no. 3, 2019, pp. 215–234. JSTOR, www.jstor.org/stable/00000000.
Notice the pieces: author (last name first), article title in quotation marks, journal name italicized, volume and issue number, year, page range, then the database name italicized and the stable URL.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND YOUR CITATIONS
An AI tool can format a citation correctly and still cite a source that does not exist, or misstate the page number of a real one. Formatting and honesty are two different problems, and an AI tool can only ever help with the first. Every citation in your Works Cited must point to something you personally opened and read, whether or not you used an AI tool to help you format it.
📚 PART 4 · TRY IT

Below are four ways to cite the same quotation from the play in-text. Three of them look plausible at first glance. Only one is actually correct.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH IN-TEXT CITATION IS CORRECT?
You are citing Tiresias’s line, “the accursed polluter of this land,” from this Gutenberg edition of the play. Which in-text citation is actually correct?
Which in-text citation is correct for this Gutenberg edition?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
“(Storr 45)” makes two errors at once: it credits the translator as though he were the play’s author, and it invents a page number this e-text edition does not have.
“(‘Oedipus the King’)” leads with the title instead of the author. MLA cites the author by name whenever the author is known, and here Sophocles is known.
“(Sophocles 45)” gets the author right but still invents a page number. This Gutenberg edition has none, so no number belongs in the citation at all.
📚 PART 5 · THE THREE WAYS CREDIT GOES MISSING

Almost no student sets out to steal someone else’s work. What actually happens is quieter: a writer means to credit a source and does it incompletely, and the finished sentence claims more than the writer intended to claim. There are exactly three ways this happens, and they are worth knowing by name, because two of the three feel perfectly honest while you are doing them.

ONE · THE UNMARKED COPY
The source’s exact words appear in your paragraph with no quotation marks around them. This one stays a problem even when you cite the source afterward, because the citation credits the idea while the missing quotation marks still present the wording as yours. Quotation marks and citations do two different jobs. You need both.
TWO · THE UNCREDITED PARAPHRASE
You put the idea entirely in your own words, correctly and well, and then you do not cite it. This is the most common one by a wide margin, and the one students are most surprised by. Rewording does not make an idea yours. If you did not think of it, it needs a citation, no matter how much of the language you changed.
THREE · THE HALF-CHANGED SENTENCE
You keep the source’s sentence and swap a few words for synonyms. The result reads like a paraphrase and functions like a copy: the structure, the order of ideas, and most of the phrasing still belong to the source. The test is simple. Close the source, then write the sentence. If you had to keep the original in front of you to produce it, it is not yet a paraphrase.
✅ THE FIX · THREE QUESTIONS BEFORE ANY BORROWED SENTENCE GOES IN
1. Are any of these words the source’s? If yes, they go inside quotation marks.
2. Is any of this thinking the source’s? If yes, it gets a citation, in your words or theirs.
3. Could I have written this sentence with the source closed? If no, rewrite it with the source closed, then check it against the original for accuracy.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH HANDLING IS ACTUALLY CLEAN?
A student is working from one sentence in the model article: Bennett writes, on page 220, that “pollution asks what was done, not what was known.” Four students handle it four ways below. Three of them have a problem. Only one is clean.
Which handling is clean?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT
The first is the unmarked copy, and it is the one most students would defend. The citation is there and it is correct, so the idea is credited. But nine of Bennett’s exact words sit in the sentence with no quotation marks, which tells the reader the phrasing is the student’s. Adding quotation marks around “pollution asks what was done, not what was known” fixes it completely.
The third is the half-changed sentence. Two words moved (“committed” for “done,” “understood” for “known”) and everything else stayed: the structure, the rhythm, the order of the two halves. A reader comparing it to Bennett would see one sentence, lightly repainted. The citation does not rescue it, because the sentence is still Bennett’s.
The fourth is the uncredited paraphrase, and notice what makes it tricky: the wording is excellent, fully the student’s own, and it is exactly the same sentence as the correct answer. The only difference is the missing citation. That one missing detail turns good work into a claim that the student thought of Bennett’s point.
📚 PART 6 · YOUR SOURCE WORK SHEET

Now do it with your own source. Open the JStor article you logged in Quick Write 13, the real one you read yourself, and work through the four steps below. Two of the steps ask for things that sound similar and are not:

A paraphrase restates one passage in your own words, at roughly the same length. Nothing is left out. You use it when the source’s point matters but the source’s exact wording does not.
A summary compresses the whole source down to its central idea, in far fewer words. Most of the detail is deliberately left out. You use it when a reader needs to know what an article argues before you start quoting from it.

Fill in the work sheet, check it, then download it. The file you download is yours to keep and paste straight into your brief, so write these as if they were going in the final draft, because they are.

YOUR NAME (FOR THE FILE NAME)

YOUR SOURCE
The author and title of the article you actually read, plus the page your passage is on.
STEP 1 · THE PASSAGE, COPIED EXACTLY
Type one or two sentences from your source exactly as they appear, word for word. Copying it accurately here is what lets you check your own paraphrase against it in a moment.

STEP 2 · YOUR PARAPHRASE OF THAT PASSAGE

In one or two sentences, what does this article argue overall? Not this passage. The whole thing. Aim for 40 words or fewer.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND PARAPHRASING
An AI tool will reword a passage on request, and the result usually reads smoothly. Two things it cannot do: confirm that the reworded version still means what the source meant, and tell you whether the idea needs a citation. It does not know which ideas are yours. A paraphrase produced by a tool and pasted in without a citation is the second problem in Part 5, whatever produced the wording.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Cite the play by its author, with no invented page number. Cite your journal article by author and page. And cite only what you have actually read yourself, in the edition you actually read it in. A citation is a small thing on the page and a large promise behind it.

The three ways credit goes missing are worth carrying past this brief. Quotation marks mark whose words these are. A citation marks whose thinking it is. You will need both for the rest of your school career, and the habit is easier to build now, on one article you have read closely, than later on twenty you have skimmed.
