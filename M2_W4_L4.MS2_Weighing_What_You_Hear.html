Weighing What You Hear
Lesson · Week 4 · Listening & Evaluation
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
On the page you can reread a sentence until you catch what is wrong with it. In a hearing, it goes past you once, and the speaker decides how fast.
Lesson 4.MS1 gave you six ways reasoning breaks and a method for objecting to them. All of that work happened on paper. This lesson takes the same skill somewhere harder. In Week 9 you will sit as a member of the Council while classmates deliver closing arguments, and you will have to weigh what you hear in real time, without a text in front of you. Listening well is a technique, not a personality trait, and it can be practiced.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain how delivery can make an argument sound stronger than its reasoning is.
2
I will be able to recognize four ways evidence gets exaggerated or distorted when it is spoken aloud.
3
I will be able to track a spoken argument on a listening sheet and turn my notes into one fair, answerable question.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT CHANGES WHEN AN ARGUMENT IS SPOKEN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a speaker who convinced you at the time, and whose argument you were less sure about an hour later. What do you think was doing the persuading while you were listening? (No wrong answer.)

A listener is at a genuine structural disadvantage, and it is worth being clear that this is not a failure of attention. Three things are true of a spoken argument that are not true of a written one. You cannot go back. You cannot control the pace. And you are receiving tone, volume, eye contact, and pauses alongside the words, all of which affect how convinced you feel without changing whether the argument is sound.

The remedy is to stop trying to evaluate while you listen, and instead record the argument’s parts as they go by, saving the judgment for afterward. The four columns below are what to write down. Separating them is what makes the fourth column possible, and the fourth column is where broken reasoning shows up.

📝 A LISTENING SHEET, FILLED IN WHILE HEARING ONE MINUTE OF ARGUMENT
The speaker: a classmate arguing that Oedipus should be found not guilty because he acted without knowledge.
1 · THE CLAIM
Not guilty. Guilt requires knowing, and he did not know.
2 · EVIDENCE OFFERED
The curse on himself (he would not curse himself if he knew). Jocasta’s account of the exposure. One scholar on pollution.
3 · REASONING CONNECTING THEM
Said quickly, once: a person cannot be blamed for what they could not have known.
4 · ASSERTED, NEVER SUPPORTED
That the Greeks shared our standard. Said three times as though agreed, never argued for. Also “every scholar accepts this.”
What the sheet made visible: columns 1 and 2 were strong, and while listening the whole thing felt strong because of them. Column 3 got one rushed sentence carrying the entire argument. Column 4 is where the case actually rests, and nothing in it was ever supported. None of that was noticeable in the moment. All of it is obvious in four columns.

Column 4 is the one students leave blank, and it is the one that matters most. If you finish listening with nothing written in it, you were almost certainly following the speaker rather than weighing them.

📚 PART 2 · FOUR WAYS SPOKEN EVIDENCE GETS STRETCHED

Broken reasoning is one problem. Overstated evidence is a different one, and it is easier to get away with out loud, because a listener cannot check anything. These four are the common moves. Notice that in all four, nothing said is outright false.

THE NUMBER WITH NO HOME
A figure arrives with no source attached. “Most scholars,” “nine out of ten historians,” “the vast majority of readings.” Numbers feel like evidence, and an unsourced number is only an assertion wearing evidence’s clothes. The question to ask is simply: according to whom, counted how?
THE QUOTATION WITH ITS QUALIFIER REMOVED
A source who wrote “this may suggest, in some cases, that intent mattered less” is quoted aloud as “intent mattered less.” Every remaining word is genuinely the source’s. The hedges that made it a careful claim are gone, and a cautious scholar has been turned into a confident one who agrees with the speaker.
THE TRUE FACT THAT DOES NOT BEAR ON IT
A verifiable, accurate detail is offered in support of a claim it does not actually touch. “Thebes was suffering terribly, with the crops failing and the dead unburied.” All true, vividly delivered, and it establishes nothing about who killed Laius. This one is hard to catch because the impulse to object feels like objecting to a fact.
THE CONSENSUS NOBODY POLLED
“Everyone accepts that…,” “no serious reader thinks…,” “we all know…” These phrases do not add evidence. They make disagreeing feel like being the only person in the room who has not understood, which is a social cost, not a logical one.
⚡ TRY IT · WHAT WENT WRONG WITH THE EVIDENCE?
A speaker says: “The scholarship is settled on this. As one leading classicist puts it, pollution operates without regard to knowledge. Thebes was dying, the fields were barren, children were going unburied in the streets. This is not a close question.” The article the speaker read actually says pollution “may, in certain ritual contexts, operate without regard to knowledge.” What is the most serious problem here?
Which is the most serious problem?
WHY THE OTHER THREE FALL SHORT AS ANSWERS
All three are real problems, and in a hearing you might raise any of them. The question asked which is most serious, and the test for that is whether the problem changes what the evidence establishes or only how it feels.
The first and second are the true-but-irrelevant fact and the unpolled consensus. Strip both out and the argument loses atmosphere and social pressure, but its actual evidence is unchanged. They inflate the delivery, not the proof.
The third is a genuine citation failure worth raising. But notice that fixing it would not help: if the classicist were named, the quotation attributed to them would still say something they did not say. The unnamed source is a problem with checking the evidence. The removed qualifier is a problem with the evidence itself.
📚 PART 3 · YOUR LISTENING SHEET

Now use it on a real speaker. Choose whichever of these you can get to:

Option A. A classmate’s recorded opening theory from the Week 5 discussion, if they posted the audio version.
Option B. Your own recorded closing-argument rehearsal. Listening to yourself is uncomfortable and unusually useful, because you know what you meant and can hear whether you actually said it.
Option C. Any recorded argument you can find on a question you care about, in or out of school.

Fill the sheet in while you listen, not afterward, and resist the urge to judge as you go. Then check it and download it. Bring it with you to the Week 9 tribunal, where you will use the same four columns on live closing arguments.

🎧 LISTENING SHEET · WEIGH A SPOKEN ARGUMENT
YOUR NAME (FOR THE FILE NAME)
WHO OR WHAT YOU LISTENED TO
COLUMN 1 · THE CLAIM
In one sentence, what did the speaker want you to accept?
COLUMN 2 · EVIDENCE OFFERED
List what they actually put forward. Quotations, sources, events, numbers. Just list it, do not judge it yet.
COLUMN 3 · THE REASONING CONNECTING THEM
What did they say to link the evidence to the claim? If they never did, write that. A blank column 3 is a finding, not a gap in your notes.
COLUMN 4 · ASSERTED BUT NEVER SUPPORTED
The one that matters. What did they state as though it were agreed, without ever arguing for it? Listen for anything repeated confidently more than twice.
DID YOU HEAR ANY OF THESE? (OPTIONAL)
IF YOU CHECKED ANYTHING ABOVE · WHAT WOULD THE ARGUMENT NEED IN ORDER TO WORK?
This is the half of an objection that does the damage. Name the specific evidence that would have to be supplied.
YOUR ONE QUESTION FOR THE SPEAKER
One question, honestly asked, that they could actually answer. Not a trap and not a speech.
Nothing here is graded and nothing is sent anywhere. The download saves a plain text file to your device, ready to bring to the tribunal.
➡️ WHAT YOU’LL DO NEXT
1
Keep the sheet you downloaded.
Save it into your ELA10 folder in OneDrive. You will want the four columns in front of you at the Week 9 tribunal, and again in VR-8.02, where you practice answering hostile questions rather than asking them.
2
Turn the sheet on yourself before Week 8.
When you rehearse your own closing argument, record it and fill in a sheet as though a stranger were speaking. Column 4 is the one to watch. What are you asserting confidently and never actually supporting?
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Do not evaluate while you listen. Record the parts, then judge afterward. Confidence, pace, and repetition all make an argument feel proved, and none of them are proof.

Fair listening is not the same as agreeable listening. The most respectful thing you can do for a speaker is take their argument seriously enough to find out exactly where it holds and exactly where it does not, and then ask them about it.
